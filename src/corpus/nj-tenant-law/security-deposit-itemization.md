# Security Deposit Itemized Deductions

## Statutory itemization requirement

When a NJ landlord withholds any portion of a security deposit at the end of a tenancy, **NJ Stat 46:8-21.1** requires the landlord to provide the tenant with a **written, itemized list of deductions** — by category and amount — within the same 30-day return window.

A bald letter saying "your deposit was applied to damages" does not satisfy the statute; the landlord must specify which damages, what they cost to repair, and what was charged.

## What counts as a permissible deduction

The landlord may deduct only for:

- **Unpaid rent** (with documentation of the period and amount).
- **Damage beyond ordinary wear and tear**, with photos, repair invoices, and itemized cost breakdown.
- **Other actual losses** caused by the tenant's documented breach of the lease.

## What is NOT a permissible deduction

- Routine cleaning between tenancies (unless the unit was left in extraordinary condition).
- Repainting between tenancies (NJ courts generally treat this as ordinary turnover cost).
- Replacement of items at full cost when only depreciated value applies (e.g., a 7-year-old carpet replaced at full cost — depreciation must be applied).
- "Administrative fees" or "lease termination fees" cloaked as security-deposit deductions.

## Tenant remedy for improper deduction

If the landlord deducts improperly or fails to itemize:

- The tenant may recover **double the wrongfully withheld amount** plus court costs and reasonable attorneys' fees.
- The doubling penalty applies even when the landlord acted in good faith but failed the procedural itemization requirement.

## Common red flags

- A move-out letter that lumps all deductions into "damages and cleaning" with no itemization.
- A deduction for repainting a unit the tenant occupied for 5+ years — treated as turnover wear in NJ.
- A deduction for replacement of fixtures at full new-replacement cost without depreciation.
- A lease clause stating the security deposit is automatically forfeit if the tenant breaks the lease — overrides the statutory framework and is unenforceable.
