# Landlord Entry — Emergencies

## Emergency exception

A landlord may enter the premises **without prior notice** in a genuine emergency that threatens life or property. Examples include:

- Fire or smoke.
- Active flooding or water main break.
- Gas leak.
- Imminent structural failure.
- Active criminal incident or 911 emergency where the landlord is responding to public-safety request.

The emergency must be real and contemporaneous; the exception does not authorize "preventive" entries the landlord later characterizes as emergencies.

## Notification after entry

Even when emergency entry is justified, the landlord should notify the tenant promptly afterward — describing the emergency, the time of entry, what was done, and any damage or repairs initiated.

## Documentation

A landlord who repeatedly invokes the emergency exception without contemporaneous documentation (photos, time-stamped notes, photos of damage, third-party first responders' presence) risks the entries being recharacterized as unauthorized.

## Common red flags

- A lease clause that defines "emergency" so broadly that it covers routine maintenance ("emergency includes any condition the landlord considers urgent").
- A clause that purports to allow emergency entry without any post-entry notice to the tenant.
- A clause that authorizes entry "for any reason during business hours" — the landlord cannot convert all entries into emergencies.
