# Repair and Deduct

## The remedy

When a landlord fails to make repairs that are the landlord's responsibility under the lease or under the implied warranty of habitability (see habitability-warranty), a New Jersey tenant may, after reasonable notice, **make the repair and deduct the reasonable cost from the next month's rent**. The remedy was established in *Marini v. Ireland*, 56 N.J. 130 (1970).

## Steps the tenant must follow

1. **Written notice** to the landlord, describing the defect and requesting repair within a reasonable time (typically 7–14 days, shorter for emergencies).
2. **Wait the reasonable cure period.** If the landlord does not act, the tenant may proceed.
3. **Obtain at least one written estimate** from a licensed contractor; preferably two.
4. **Make the repair using a licensed, insured contractor** — not a friend or self-help.
5. **Deduct the actual, documented cost** from the next rent payment. Provide the landlord with a copy of the invoice.

A self-help repair without notice and opportunity to cure may forfeit the remedy.

## Limits

- The repair must be of a defect that is the landlord's responsibility, not the tenant's.
- The cost must be reasonable — three written estimates are the safest record.
- The repair must be necessary for habitability or required by the lease; cosmetic upgrades are not deductible.
- The deduction may not exceed the rent due. Larger repairs may require court action.

## Common red flags

- A lease clause that prohibits repair-and-deduct remedies or requires landlord written consent before any repair is made — unenforceable as against the warranty of habitability.
- A clause that defines tenant repair responsibility broadly (e.g., "all repairs less than $500") to push major-system maintenance off the landlord — courts will read these narrowly.
- A clause requiring the tenant to use a contractor selected solely by the landlord — unreasonable interference with self-help remedy.
