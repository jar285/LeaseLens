# Implied Warranty of Habitability

## Marini v. Ireland

In **Marini v. Ireland**, 56 N.J. 130 (1970), the New Jersey Supreme Court held that every residential lease carries an **implied warranty of habitability** — the landlord warrants that the premises are fit for human habitation when the tenant takes possession and remain so throughout the tenancy.

The warranty is implied by law and **cannot be waived** by a lease provision. A clause that purports to disclaim the warranty is unenforceable.

## What "habitable" means

A unit is habitable when it complies with applicable housing codes and provides:

- Working plumbing, hot and cold water.
- Working heat (60°F minimum from October 1 through May 1 per most NJ municipalities).
- Working electrical service.
- Weather-tight walls, roof, windows, and doors.
- Freedom from severe pest infestation.
- Working kitchen facilities (where leased as such).
- Working bathroom facilities.
- Working smoke detectors and CO detectors as required by NJ Uniform Fire Code.

## Tenant remedies for breach

When the landlord breaches the warranty of habitability and fails to repair after reasonable notice, the tenant may:

- **Repair and deduct** — make a reasonable repair and deduct the cost from rent (see repair-and-deduct).
- **Withhold rent** — pay rent into court while the landlord cures (subject to procedural rules).
- **Terminate the lease** — vacate and treat the lease as constructively terminated when the breach is so severe that the unit is uninhabitable.
- **Sue for damages** — recover the difference between the rent paid and the fair rental value of the premises in their actual condition.

## Common red flags

- A clause that disclaims the warranty of habitability or attempts to make the tenant responsible for major-system repairs (heat, plumbing, electrical).
- A clause that requires the tenant to "accept the unit AS IS" with no remedy for hidden defects.
- A clause that bars the tenant from withholding rent or pursuing repair-and-deduct remedies.
