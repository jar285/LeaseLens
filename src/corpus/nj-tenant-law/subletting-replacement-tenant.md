# Subletting — Finding a Replacement Tenant

## Practical mitigation pathway

When a tenant must vacate before the lease expires, presenting a financially qualified replacement tenant is often the cleanest path to avoid an early-termination penalty. NJ landlords are obligated by *Sommer v. Kridel* to mitigate damages; accepting a qualified replacement is a primary mitigation step.

## What "qualified" means

A replacement tenant is generally considered qualified when they:

- Pass the same credit, income, and reference screening the landlord would apply to a new applicant.
- Sign the lease (or a new lease for the remaining term) on the same financial terms.
- Have no documented history of lease defaults or property damage.

A landlord who rejects a qualified replacement without a good-faith reason undermines the landlord's mitigation defense and may forfeit the right to recover from the original tenant for the remaining term.

## Process

The original tenant should:

1. Notify the landlord in writing of the intent to sublet or assign and identify the proposed replacement.
2. Provide the replacement's income documentation and references.
3. Request a written response within a reasonable time (10-14 days).

If the landlord does not respond, or rejects without articulating specific reasons, the tenant has a stronger record for any subsequent dispute.

## Common red flags

- A lease clause that requires the original tenant to remain liable on the lease even after a landlord-approved sublet, with no termination of the original tenant's obligations.
- A clause that imposes a "subletting fee" that exceeds reasonable application-screening costs (typically $30–50 per applicant).
- A clause that gives the landlord 60+ days to respond to a sublet request — likely unreasonable in a market with reasonable turnover times.
