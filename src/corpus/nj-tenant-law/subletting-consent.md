# Subletting and Assignment — Consent

## Default rule

Absent a lease provision to the contrary, a New Jersey residential tenant generally has the right to sublet or assign the leasehold subject to the landlord's reasonable consent. NJ does not have a statute that broadly prohibits subletting; the analysis is contractual and common-law.

## Lease prohibitions

Most NJ residential leases include a clause prohibiting subletting or assignment without the landlord's prior written consent. These clauses are generally enforceable, but courts read them narrowly and require the landlord to act in good faith.

## Reasonable consent standard

Where the lease conditions subletting on landlord consent, a reasonable-consent standard is implied. The landlord may not unreasonably withhold consent — for example, refusing a financially qualified replacement tenant for arbitrary reasons.

## Anti-discrimination overlay

The landlord's evaluation of a proposed subtenant is subject to federal and NJ fair-housing laws. The landlord may not refuse a subtenant on the basis of race, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, disability, or any class protected by **NJ Stat 10:5-12** (NJ Law Against Discrimination).

## Common red flags

- A blanket prohibition on subletting "for any reason" with no consent path — courts may treat this as an unreasonable restraint.
- A clause requiring tenants to pay a flat "subletting fee" disproportionate to actual landlord costs.
- A clause that conditions consent on the subtenant's race, religion, family status, or other protected characteristic.
- A clause that grants the landlord sole and arbitrary discretion to deny — likely unenforceable as a matter of good faith.
