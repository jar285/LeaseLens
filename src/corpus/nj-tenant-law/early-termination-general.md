# Early Termination — General Tenancies

## Lease controls; reasonableness limits apply

In a typical New Jersey residential tenancy, early termination is governed by the lease. The most common arrangement is an "early termination fee" of one to two months' rent.

A clause that demands more than two months' rent as a forfeit, or that imposes liquidated damages plus a duty to pay rent through the end of the term, is likely an unenforceable penalty.

## Landlord's duty to mitigate

Even when a tenant breaks a lease without statutory protection, a NJ landlord has a **duty to mitigate damages**: the landlord must make reasonable efforts to re-rent the unit at a reasonable rent. The tenant's liability is limited to actual damages — typically the rent for the period the unit sits vacant despite reasonable re-rental efforts, plus reasonable advertising costs.

The duty was confirmed in *Sommer v. Kridel*, 74 N.J. 446 (1977), and binds residential landlords as a matter of common law.

## Permitted early-termination fees

A modest, fixed fee (one to two months' rent) functioning as a reasonable estimate of the landlord's expected loss from the early break is generally enforceable as liquidated damages. The fee should bear a real relationship to anticipated losses; an arbitrary "three months' rent" punishment likely does not.

## Common red flags

- An early-termination fee greater than two months' rent.
- A clause that requires the tenant to pay rent through the end of the lease term **in addition** to a flat fee — that's stacking, and it ignores the duty to mitigate.
- A clause that disclaims the landlord's duty to mitigate damages — unenforceable in residential leases.
- A fee that varies based on how much of the lease term remains, in a way that increases penalties as time goes on.

For senior, disabled, or domestic-violence-victim tenants, see the dedicated files — they have statutory rights to terminate without penalty.
